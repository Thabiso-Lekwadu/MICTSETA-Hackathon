## Roughness 
Road roughness is the metric that measures the variations, bumps, and irregularities along a road surface [2.1].In logistics and transport engineering, it is not just a measure of comfort; it is a critical data point that dictates vehicle wear, fuel consumption, speed degradation, and cold-chain cargo safety [2.2, 2.3].How Road Roughness is Formally MeasuredIn real-world data science and asset management, road roughness is measured using the International Roughness Index (IRI) [2.1].The Scale: It is calculated in meters of vertical vehicle movement per kilometer driven (m/km).Low IRI (0.5 to 2.0 m/km): Perfect, smooth, high-speed national tar highways (like the N1 or N14).Moderate IRI (2.0 to 5.0 m/km): Older paved roads with minor cracks, potholes, or patch repairs.High IRI (5.0 to 15.0+ m/km): Highly unpaved gravel tracks and severely corrugated dirt roads [2.1].Why Roughness Matters for Your Fisheries ModelWhen you pitch your optimizer to the hackathon judges, connect road roughness directly to your Cold-Chain Vulnerability Index (CCVI) using these three impacts:Kinetic Energy Transfer: High road roughness acts as a continuous hammer hitting the truck [2.2]. ## Snapping
In geospatial network modeling, "snapping" means taking an arbitrary coordinate point on the earth (like a single GPS ping from a vehicle or a fishing harbor) and mathematically moving it to the nearest physical road edge or intersection node in your routing graph [2.4].Because of how spatial data works, a truck's GPS device or a fishery's front gate is almost never located exactly on the centerline of an OpenStreetMap vector [2.4]. If you tried to calculate a route from those raw coordinates, your algorithm would fail because the starting point is technically sitting in an "empty field" outside the network graph.Here is exactly how snapping works under the hood in your project, based on the parameters in your nc_road_graph.pkl file:1. Distance Threshold Snapping (Connecting Nodes)When your data audit script originally built the graph network, it used a 25-meter snapping tolerance to join adjacent roads together.The Problem: In crowd-sourced data, different people map different roads. Sometimes, a primary road and a secondary road meet at an intersection, but the digitizer left a microscopic gap between them (e.g., a 2-meter gap).The Action: The code scans the network and automatically "snaps" (merges) any endpoints that fall within 25 meters of each other into a single, unified intersection node. This converts thousands of disconnected line paths into a single, flowing grid.2. Location Snapping (Mapping Places to Roads)When you look up a town or a trade checkpoint (like your town connectivity check), it is represented by a single point. ## Edge Weights of Segments (Roads)
The word impedance simply means the total friction, effort, or cost required to traverse a network segment (edge). It moves beyond simple distance (kilometers) and combines multiple real-world penalties—such as travel time, road roughness, administrative delays, and environmental hazards—into a single numeric value.1. The Core Concept: From Map to Cost Matrix When you build a basic routing model, the impedance of a road segment is usually just its physical length or standard travel time.However, in an advanced Impedance Matrix Model, you treat every road segment as a function of multiple competing weights. For your Transport, Trade, and Fisheries hackathon project, your segment impedance (Z) can be modeled mathematically like this:Z_segment=Travel Time+ Roughness Penalty+Border Delay Penalty, When you calculate this formula for every single one of your 121,552 road segments, you generate a massive matrix (array) of costs. Shortest-path solvers like Dijkstra or A* scan this impedance matrix to find the path that minimizes total resistance, rather than just minimizing physical distance.
